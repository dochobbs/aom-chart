A 6-month-old girl is brought to clinic by her father after a shaking episode at home this morning.

She has had a runny nose and mild cough for 2 days. This morning while on the play mat she stiffened, then her arms and legs jerked rhythmically; father says both sides. He started timing partway through; his phone shows 9 minutes from when he began until it stopped on its own. She was sleepy for about 20 minutes afterward and has since nursed and is looking around. No vomiting, no rash. He thought she felt warm before the episode and gave acetaminophen after. Temperature at home 38.6°C (101.5°F). Clinic temperature is 38.9°C (102.0°F). No drug allergies. Weight 7.6 kg. Otherwise healthy.

Exam: alert, tracks, consolable, smiles at father. HR 142, RR 34, SpO2 99% RA. Anterior fontanelle soft and flat. Neck supple. TMs normal. Clear rhinorrhea. No rash, no petechiae. Moves all limbs symmetrically, tone normal, no focal findings. Remainder of exam unremarkable.

Assessment

This is most consistent with a first febrile seizure associated with a viral URI, but I would not confidently label it simple yet. A simple febrile seizure must be generalized, occur once in 24 hours, and last <15 minutes. Here, the father started timing after the seizure had already begun, so the true duration is unknown and could have crossed the complex-seizure threshold. 
PEDIATRICS
+1

She is currently reassuring: back to neurologic baseline, well appearing, no focal deficit, no meningismus, and no petechiae.

Workup

Clarify actual seizure duration as best as possible, recurrence, prior afebrile seizures, developmental history, family seizure history, recent antibiotics, and—particularly because she is exactly 6 months old—Hib and pneumococcal immunization status. 
American Family Physician

Focus testing on the source of fever, not the seizure itself. The current exam favors a viral respiratory infection. 
PEDIATRICS

No routine CBC, electrolytes, glucose, EEG, CT, or MRI if this can reasonably be established as a simple febrile seizure and she remains well appearing. 
PEDIATRICS

Lumbar puncture is not routinely indicated with this reassuring examination. However, at 6–12 months, LP is an option if Hib/pneumococcal immunization is incomplete or unknown, or if she has been pretreated with antibiotics. LP becomes indicated if meningitis/encephalitis is clinically suspected. 
PEDIATRICS

Even if the event ultimately qualifies as complex solely because the duration was ≥15 minutes, emergent neuroimaging is usually unnecessary in a child who has returned to baseline with a normal neurologic examination. 
Pediatrics

Management

Observe in clinic/ED until she remains clearly at baseline, is feeding adequately, and has no recurrent seizure or evolving neurologic findings.

Treat fever for comfort, not to prevent another seizure; antipyretics do not reliably prevent febrile-seizure recurrence. 
PEDIATRICS

No maintenance antiseizure medication after an isolated otherwise uncomplicated febrile seizure. 
PEDIATRICS

Parent seizure first aid: place her on her side on a safe surface, do not restrain her or put anything in her mouth, and time the event. A seizure persisting ≥5 minutes requires emergency treatment/EMS activation because prolonged seizures should be treated with a benzodiazepine in an acute-care setting. 
American Family Physician

Disposition

If immunizations are current, she remains completely at baseline, no further seizure occurs during observation, and there is no concern for CNS infection, discharge with close pediatric follow-up is reasonable. Because the true seizure duration is uncertain and she is at the very lower age boundary for febrile seizures, I would have a lower threshold for ED observation/evaluation rather than treating this as an unequivocal routine simple febrile seizure. Guidelines and reviews commonly use young age and complex features as factors favoring additional observation. 
Pediatric Neurology
+1

When to escalate

Immediate ED/EMS evaluation for recurrent seizure, seizure lasting ≥5 minutes, persistent altered mental status, focal weakness, meningismus, petechial/purpuric rash, respiratory compromise, poor feeding/dehydration, or increasingly ill appearance.
