Assessment

The focal physical examination findings (crackles with decreased breath sounds at the right base) support lobar consolidation. The absence of wheezing, retractions, or nasal flaring is reassuring, but the moderate severity classification is driven by the SpO2 of 93% on room air. Supportive care:

Oxygen via nasal cannula 0.5–2 L/min to maintain SpO2 ≥ 94%
Antipyretics: ibuprofen 10 mg/kg every 6–8 hours PRN (mother is already using appropriately)
Encourage oral hydration; avoid IV fluids unless unable to tolerate PO
Nonpharmacologic Interventions

Position with head elevated 30–45 degrees to optimize ventilation. Small frequent sips of oral fluids to maintain hydration. Nasal saline drops and suction if congestion develops. Room humidification may improve cough comfort.

What to Avoid
Routine chest physiotherapy — not beneficial in uncomplicated pneumonia and may cause discomfort.
Systemic corticosteroids — not recommended in uncomplicated pediatric CAP unless concurrent asthma exacerbation or severe disease with inflammatory component.
Empiric azithromycin as first-line in this age group unless there is specific epidemiologic concern for Mycoplasma (older contacts, persistent cough >5 days).
Overnight pulse oximetry or prolonged admission if SpO2 stabilizes ≥ 94% on room air within 12–24 hours and clinical course improves.
Procedural Interventions
Nasal cannula oxygen as needed for SpO2 ≥ 94%.
Peripheral IV placement for fluids/medications if oral intake becomes inadequate.
Thoracentesis/chest tube if chest radiograph reveals significant parapneumonic effusion with respiratory compromise or failure to improve

Disposition

Admit to pediatric ward for 12–24 hours of observation with supplemental oxygen capability. Discharge criteria include:

SpO2 ≥ 94% on room air for ≥ 4–6 hours without oxygen requirement
Tolerating oral intake
Afebrile or stable temperature with antipyretics
Adequate home support and ability to return if deterioration

If home support is inadequate, social barriers exist, or oxygen requirement persists beyond 12–24 hours, extend admission.

Red Flags

Require immediate reassessment or escalation to higher level of care:

SpO2 declining to < 90% despite supplemental oxygen
Development of retractions, nasal flaring, or grunting
Altered mental status or lethargy
Inability to tolerate oral fluids
Persistent fever >72 hours after initiating antibiotics
Asymmetrical chest expansion or new focal findings suggesting effusion
Tachycardia >160 or signs of hemodynamic instability
Follow-up

Reassess within 24–48 hours after discharge or sooner if red flags develop. If symptoms worsen or fail to improve by day 3–4 of antibiotics, obtain repeat chest radiograph to evaluate for parapneumonic effusion or necrotizing pneumonia. Post-treatment chest radiograph is not routinely required if clinical improvement occurs, as radiographic resolution lags clinical recovery by 4–6 weeks.

Patient Education

Counsel the mother that:

Cough typically persists 2–4 weeks even with appropriate treatment
Fever should improve within 48–72 hours of antibiotics
Complete the full antibiotic course even if symptoms improve earlier
Return immediately for breathing difficulty, lethargy, refusal to drink, or persistent high fever
Household contacts should practice hand hygiene; children with pneumonia may return to school after 24 hours of antibiotics and fever-free for 24 hours
Common Pitfalls
Anchoring on viral etiology and delaying antibiotics in a child with focal lobar findings and hypoxemia
Discharging a hypoxemic patient without ensuring SpO2 ≥ 94% on room air and adequate home monitoring
Missing parapneumonic effusion by not obtaining chest radiograph when severity criteria are met or clinical course worsens
Underdosing amoxicillin — standard doses (45 mg/kg/day) may be insufficient for penicillin-resistant pneumococcus; high-dose (90 mg/kg/day) is guideline-preferred for CAP
