This presentation meets diagnostic criteria for acute otitis media because there is middle ear effusion plus acute inflammation, with a bulging tympanic membrane. Management should prioritize pain control and then use shared decision-making about initial observation with analgesia versus immediate antibiotics, since the child is not in a high-risk group. High-risk features that would push toward immediate antibiotics include age under 6 months, immunocompromise, toxic appearance, or craniofacial abnormalities.

Initial management choice

For most children who are not at increased risk, initial observation with analgesia is suggested rather than immediate antibiotics because many cases improve within about three days and antibiotics have modest absolute benefit with common adverse effects. However, children who are younger than 2 years, have more severe manifestations, bilateral AOM, severe bulging, otorrhea, tympanic membrane perforation, or symptoms for more than 72 hours are more likely to benefit from immediate antibiotics.
+1

If observation is chosen, a concrete contingency plan is needed, either with no prescription plus clear after-hours instructions, or a delayed prescription to fill only if the child worsens or does not improve within 72 hours. Examples of “worsening” that can be operationalized include fever above 39°C, pain not improved with oral analgesics, new ear drainage, worsening fussiness, or lack of improvement by 72 hours.

Analgesia

Pain management is a mainstay of AOM care; oral ibuprofen or acetaminophen are suggested. Topical anesthetic drops are options only with an intact tympanic membrane, and topical benzocaine should not be used in children under 2 years due to methemoglobinemia risk.
+1

If antibiotics are started now or after failed observation

For most children, high-dose amoxicillin is suggested as initial therapy. If the child received a beta-lactam antibiotic in the prior 30 days or has recurrent AOM unresponsive to amoxicillin, high-dose amoxicillin-clavulanate is suggested instead. If symptoms worsen or fail to improve after 72 hours of observation, antibiotics should be started; if symptoms worsen or fail to improve after 72 hours of antibiotics, switch to a second-line antimicrobial agent and reassess for complications or an alternate diagnosis.

Follow-up and escalation

Routine follow-up is generally not needed after symptom resolution, but children with recurrent infections, chronic hearing loss, or language or learning problems should be reexamined in 8 to 12 weeks to ensure effusion resolution. If mastoiditis is suspected, imaging is not needed when classic findings are present, but may be needed when findings are atypical or complications are suspected.
