Analyzed query, searched for evidence

Outpatient treatment with a 7–10 day course of an oral antibiotic is appropriate for this well-appearing, well-hydrated 24-month-old with a first febrile UTI. The catheterized specimen (LE 2+, nitrite positive, 30 WBC/hpf, many bacteria) plus fever meets criteria for UTI, and because he is febrile without an alternative source, this should be treated as presumed pyelonephritis. He tolerates oral intake, has normal vitals and perfusion, and is drinking well — so hospitalization and parenteral therapy are not required. Children older than 2 months who can take oral medication are safely managed with oral antibiotics alone, and a single parenteral dose is not needed before discharge.

Antibiotic selection

Choose empiric therapy guided by your local antibiogram. In young febrile children, choose an agent with low regional resistance. Third-generation cephalosporins have very high uropathogen susceptibility; amoxicillin alone should be avoided because of high E. coli resistance.

Reasonable oral options per AAP include an oral third-generation cephalosporin (cefdinir, cefixime, cefpodoxime), cephalexin (if local UPEC susceptibility is favorable), amoxicillin-clavulanate, or TMP-SMX. Nitrofurantoin should not be used for presumed pyelonephritis because it does not achieve adequate renal tissue levels.

[4]

Narrow therapy once culture and susceptibilities return. No test-of-cure urinalysis or culture is needed if he improves.

Duration

7–10 days is standard for uncomplicated febrile UTI presumed to be pyelonephritis with good clinical response. Emerging RCT data (e.g., a 5-day noninferiority trial and individualized-duration trials) suggest shorter courses may be adequate in well-appearing children, but guideline-endorsed standard remains 7–10 days.

Supportive care and follow-up

Antipyretics (acetaminophen ~15 mg/kg) for fever/discomfort.

Expect defervescence within 24–72 hours; fever resolves in ~68% by 24 h and ~92% by 72 h. Advise the mother to return if fever persists beyond 48–72 hours, if he becomes toxic, stops drinking, has decreased urine output, or vomits medications — persistent fever should prompt reevaluation and consideration of renal ultrasound to exclude abscess or obstruction. 
Pediatrics
[1]

Ensure he starts antibiotics promptly (within 48 h of fever onset), as delay increases risk of renal scarring. 
Pediatrics
[1]

Imaging

Obtain a renal and bladder ultrasound (RBUS) after this first febrile UTI, since he is ≤24 months. It can be done as an outpatient after acute symptoms resolve (typically within 6 months) to reduce false-positive dilation from acute inflammation; do it acutely only if the course is atypical or fever persists >48–72 h.
