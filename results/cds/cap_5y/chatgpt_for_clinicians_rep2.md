A 5-year-old girl is brought to clinic by her mother for cough and fever.

She has had a cough for 3 days, worse at night, and fever for 2 days. Mother gave ibuprofen this morning. She is eating less but drinking well. No vomiting. Mother thinks she is breathing "a little fast." No history of wheezing. Clinic temperature is 38.8°C (101.8°F). Immunizations up to date. No drug allergies. Weight 18.5 kg. Otherwise healthy.

Exam: alert, mildly tired-appearing, talking in full sentences. HR 122, RR 38, SpO2 93% RA. No retractions, no nasal flaring. Crackles at the right base with decreased breath sounds there. No wheeze. Rest of chest clear. TMs normal. Throat mildly injected. Capillary refill under 2 seconds. Remainder of exam unremarkable.

What is your plan?

Assessment

This is community-acquired pneumonia, likely right lower lobe, with focal crackles/decreased breath sounds, fever, and mild hypoxemia. The SpO₂ of 93% on room air is the main disposition issue. Hypoxemia is associated with greater CAP severity, and some pediatric guidance uses values in the 90–93% range as a reason for a lower threshold for hospital evaluation. 
The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health
+1

Management

Repeat the pulse oximetry after she has rested, ensuring a good waveform/probe position. If the saturation remains around 93%, I would send her to the ED for further evaluation rather than routine outpatient management, particularly given the focal decreased breath sounds. Definite hospitalization is recommended with sustained room-air SpO₂ <90%, but the 90–93% range warrants individualized disposition based on trajectory and exam. 
The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health

Obtain a chest radiograph given the abnormal oxygenation and focal decreased breath sounds. Imaging is specifically recommended when hypoxemia or significant respiratory distress is present and is useful here to assess consolidation and a possible parapneumonic effusion. 
Clinical Infectious Diseases
 If an effusion is seen and is moderate/large, chest ultrasound is preferred for further characterization. 
IDSA

If she is ultimately judged stable for outpatient treatment and bacterial CAP remains the working diagnosis, amoxicillin is first-line. A commonly recommended regimen for children ≥5 years is 90 mg/kg/day PO divided BID; current evidence supports a 5-day course for uncomplicated outpatient CAP when there is appropriate clinical improvement. 
Clinical Infectious Diseases
+1
 I would use a validated pediatric dosing calculator to convert her 18.5-kg weight to the prescription dose rather than hand-calculating it here.

I would not automatically add azithromycin. At age 5, Mycoplasma becomes more relevant, but her abrupt febrile illness with focal right-basilar findings fits typical bacterial pneumonia reasonably well. Add atypical coverage if the history, radiograph, local epidemiology, or clinical course makes atypical CAP more likely. 
Clinical Infectious Diseases

Supportive treatment: fluids as tolerated, antipyretic/analgesic therapy as needed, and no bronchodilator in the absence of wheezing/bronchospasm.

Monitoring

If discharged after reassuring reassessment, arrange close follow-up within 24–48 hours. Lack of improvement or clinical deterioration within 48–72 hours should trigger reevaluation for resistant/incorrect pathogen, effusion/empyema, necrotizing pneumonia, or another diagnosis. 
Clinical Infectious Diseases
+1

When to escalate

Persistent or worsening hypoxemia, increasing work of breathing, inability to maintain hydration, altered mental status, or clinical deterioration warrants ED evaluation/admission. In this particular case, if the repeat room-air SpO₂ stays at 93%, my preference is ED evaluation rather than sending her straight home on antibiotics.
