This episode should be treated as a prolonged complex febrile seizure—approximately 9 minutes, at 6 months of age—requiring same-day emergency-department evaluation, not routine outpatient observation. She is currently well-appearing and neurologically normal, but the duration and incompletely documented immunization status warrant evaluation for CNS infection and other acute causes.




Immediate disposition




Activate EMS/ED transfer now. Do not have the family transport privately if transfer can be arranged by EMS.
During transfer, monitor airway, breathing, circulation, mental status, temperature, and glucose; establish IV/IO access if needed.
If seizure recurs and persists for ≥5 minutes, treat as convulsive status epilepticus per local protocol with weight-based benzodiazepine; for 7.6 kg, examples include midazolam 0.2 mg/kg intranasally/buccally or lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg IV/IO, subject to local formulation and maximum-dose protocols. Avoid repeated benzodiazepine dosing beyond protocol because of respiratory-depression risk. 




ED evaluation




Reassess seizure semiology, exact duration, focality, recurrence, postictal course, fever timing, medications, exposures, trauma, developmental history, and family history of seizures.
Check point-of-care glucose promptly; obtain additional laboratory testing only as clinically indicated by appearance, hydration, persistent altered mental status, recurrence, or suspected metabolic/toxic cause.
Review immunization records urgently, specifically Hib and pneumococcal series. At 6–12 months, lumbar puncture is an option when immunization status is incomplete or cannot be determined; it is indicated if meningitis or encephalitis is clinically suspected. 
Given the prolonged seizure and fever, ED clinicians should have a low threshold for LP after stabilization if mental status does not return to baseline, meningeal signs develop, fever lacks a reassuring source, or immunizations are incomplete/unknown. Do not perform LP before stabilizing airway, breathing, circulation, or controlling ongoing seizures.
No routine EEG or neuroimaging is indicated solely because this was a febrile seizure in an otherwise normal child; imaging becomes appropriate for persistent focal deficit, trauma, signs of raised intracranial pressure, markedly abnormal mental status, or another concerning feature. 
Evaluate the respiratory illness and fever source clinically; avoid attributing the fever to a viral URI until serious bacterial infection has been reasonably excluded.




Counseling




Position her on her side during any seizure, remove nearby hazards, do not restrain her or place anything in her mouth, and time the event from onset.
Call EMS immediately for seizure lasting 5 minutes, repeated seizures without recovery, breathing or color change, focal seizure activity, persistent abnormal responsiveness, petechiae, neck stiffness, or clinical deterioration.
Acetaminophen may improve comfort but does not reliably prevent recurrent febrile seizures; use antipyretics for distress rather than to normalize the temperature. 




The key missing fact is her Hib and pneumococcal immunization status, which directly affects the threshold for lumbar puncture.
