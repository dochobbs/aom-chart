# Glass Health — First Febrile UTI (24 Months) (Rep 2)

This 24-month-old is well appearing, hydrated, and has a catheterized UA strongly supporting a febrile UTI, clinically likely lower-tract infection versus early pyelonephritis. Start empiric oral therapy today, pending culture and susceptibility results; no imaging, blood cultures, or admission is indicated presently.





Febrile urinary tract infection




The positive nitrite, pyuria, and bacteriuria from a catheterized specimen support UTI. He is tolerating oral intake without vomiting, has normal perfusion and oxygenation, and lacks examination findings suggesting sepsis or obstruction. Oral treatment is appropriate when reliable intake and outpatient follow-up are possible; febrile UTI is generally treated for 7–10 days. [1][3]





Dx.

Follow the catheterized urine culture and susceptibility results; contact the family within 48–72 hours to narrow or change therapy.
No routine blood testing or blood culture now given well appearance, stable vital signs, preserved intake, and no toxicity.
No immediate renal/bladder ultrasound or VCUG for an uncomplicated first febrile UTI responding to treatment. Reconsider imaging for atypical course, poor response, non-E. coli organism, recurrent febrile UTI, abnormal examination, or concern for obstruction. [2]




Tx.

Start cephalexin 25 mg/kg/dose PO three times daily for 7 days: 315 mg PO TID using the prescribed liquid concentration, unless local resistance history or prior cultures favor another agent.
Adjust or discontinue therapy when culture and susceptibility results return; reassess antibiotic choice if fever or clinical status is not improving by 48 hours.
Give acetaminophen 15 mg/kg PO every 4–6 hours as needed for discomfort or fever: 189 mg per dose; do not exceed 5 doses in 24 hours.
Maintain oral fluids and usual breastfeeding/milk intake; monitor urine output.
Seek urgent evaluation for lethargy, worsening appearance, persistent vomiting, inability to take oral medication or fluids, reduced urine output, breathing difficulty, flank pain, or signs of dehydration.
Re-examine or arrange urgent reassessment if fever persists beyond 48 hours after starting an active antibiotic, with repeat urine evaluation/culture and consideration of renal/bladder imaging or parenteral therapy.
Follow up by telephone when culture results are available and in clinic if symptoms are not clearly improving within 48–72 hours. Further routine follow-up is not required if he improves and this is a first uncomplicated episode. [3]




Before prescribing, verify the local antibiogram, formulation concentration, renal function if clinically indicated, and absence of a prior resistant urine isolate.
