A 24-month-old boy is brought to clinic by his father after a fall at home.

About 2 hours ago he fell off the living-room couch onto a hardwood floor. Father was in the kitchen and heard the thud; the boy was crying when he got to him. He vomited once in the car on the way in. No seizure activity. Immunizations up to date. No drug allergies. Weight 12.8 kg. Otherwise healthy.

Exam: alert, walks to the toy bin and plays, GCS 15. HR 112, RR 24, SpO2 99% RA. 3 cm soft boggy swelling over the occiput, no step-off, no laceration. Pupils equal and reactive. No hemotympanum, no Battle sign, no periorbital bruising. Fontanelle closed. No bruising elsewhere on full skin exam. Neuro non-focal, gait normal. Remainder of exam unremarkable.

What is your plan?

Searched evidence

Send him to a pediatric emergency department for structured observation and serial neurologic examinations; based on the current findings, do not obtain an immediate head computed tomography (CT) solely for one isolated emesis.

CLINICAL GUIDELINE

Use the age-appropriate Pediatric Emergency Care Applied Research Network (PECARN) algorithm; for children at intermediate risk—especially with isolated findings—initial observation is preferred over immediate CT.

Assessment

At exactly 24 months, use the PECARN branch for children ≥2 years.
He has normal mental status, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) 15, normal neurologic examination, no seizure, no clinical basilar skull fracture, and apparently nonsevere mechanism.
Vomiting once is an intermediate-risk feature; isolated vomiting is associated with a very low frequency of clinically important traumatic brain injury, making observation reasonable rather than automatic CT. 
PEDIATRICS
2018
The occipital hematoma is a PECARN predictor primarily in children <2 years; at exactly 24 months it is not, by itself, a CT indication. 
BMJ Open
2024

Immediate plan

Confirm the exact fall height, loss of consciousness, abnormal behavior, worsening headache, anticoagulant/bleeding history, and whether the child is acting normally for his father.
Observe with repeated age-appropriate mental-status, pupil, gait, and neurologic assessments; monitor vomiting and vital signs. Because he is currently in clinic, transfer to an ED unless equivalent monitored observation and emergent CT capability are available.
Obtain urgent CT and consult pediatric trauma/neurosurgery if he develops GCS <15 or altered behavior, recurrent vomiting, worsening headache, seizure, focal deficit, palpable/depressed skull fracture, basilar skull signs, or clinical deterioration.
If he remains neurologically normal, has no further emesis, tolerates fluids, and reliable caregivers can return promptly, discharge with written head-injury precautions. Reliable caregivers, easy access to emergency care, and no other injury requiring admission are appropriate discharge requirements.
