This is a complex febrile seizure because it lasted approximately 9 minutes. Although she is currently well appearing, a first prolonged seizure in a 6-month-old warrants immediate emergency-department evaluation by EMS, not outpatient observation. A seizure lasting ≥5 minutes should be treated as prolonged/status epilepticus, and children without a prior seizure history require hospital assessment. [1][3]





Activate EMS now; maintain airway, place her lateral if somnolent, avoid oral intake during transport, and send the father’s seizure video/timing information.
Check point-of-care glucose if immediately available without delaying transport; establish monitoring and reassess mental status, perfusion, and neurologic findings.
If seizure recurs and lasts ≥5 minutes: administer the clinic’s weight-based benzodiazepine protocol. At 7.6 kg, the exact product, route, concentration, and available resuscitation support must be verified before dosing; provide oxygen and airway support as needed.
In the ED, evaluate for the fever source and seizure precipitants. Consider CBC/electrolytes/glucose, cultures, and other testing based on clinical appearance and findings—not routinely for a simple febrile seizure.
Because she is 6 months old and immunization status is undocumented, lumbar puncture is an option if Hib/pneumococcal immunization cannot be verified; it is indicated with meningismus, persistent altered mental status, toxic appearance, or other concern for meningitis. [2]
Routine CT, MRI, or EEG is not indicated solely on the current normal examination, but neuroimaging/EEG should be guided by persistent altered consciousness, focal findings, recurrent seizures, concern for increased intracranial pressure, trauma, or failure to return to baseline. [2][3]




Do not prescribe prophylactic daily antiseizure medication from clinic. Antipyretics may improve comfort but do not reliably prevent recurrent febrile seizures. Verify immunization history and arrange pediatric/neurology follow-up after acute evaluation.
