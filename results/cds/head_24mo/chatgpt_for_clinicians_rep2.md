A 24-month-old boy is brought to clinic by his father after a fall at home.

About 2 hours ago he fell off the living-room couch onto a hardwood floor. Father was in the kitchen and heard the thud; the boy was crying when he got to him. He vomited once in the car on the way in. No seizure activity. Immunizations up to date. No drug allergies. Weight 12.8 kg. Otherwise healthy.

Exam: alert, walks to the toy bin and plays, GCS 15. HR 112, RR 24, SpO2 99% RA. 3 cm soft boggy swelling over the occiput, no step-off, no laceration. Pupils equal and reactive. No hemotympanum, no Battle sign, no periorbital bruising. Fontanelle closed. No bruising elsewhere on full skin exam. Neuro non-focal, gait normal. Remainder of exam unremarkable.

What is your plan?

Assessment

This is minor blunt head trauma with a normal neurologic examination, but he is not PECARN very-low-risk because at exactly 24 months he falls into the “age ≥2 years” rule, and post-traumatic vomiting is an intermediate-risk predictor. The occipital hematoma is notable clinically, but nonfrontal scalp hematoma is a PECARN predictor only in children <2 years. 
The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health
+1

Management

Favor observation rather than immediate CT. He has GCS 15, no altered mental status, no basilar skull-fracture signs, no focal deficit, and the couch fall is not a PECARN severe mechanism assuming it was <5 ft. His single vomiting episode is therefore essentially an isolated intermediate-risk feature. PECARN supports observation versus CT, with observation particularly reasonable for isolated findings. 
ACEP
+1

I would observe until approximately 4–6 hours from the time of injury, with serial mental-status/GCS, pupils, gait/neurologic examination, and monitoring for recurrent vomiting, worsening headache, abnormal behavior, somnolence, or other deterioration. 
Italian Journal of Pediatrics

Do not obtain a routine head CT now if he remains clinically well. CT becomes appropriate if symptoms worsen during observation—particularly recurrent/persistent vomiting, altered mental status, worsening headache, focal findings, or other concern for intracranial injury. Routine neuroimaging for pediatric mTBI is discouraged when a validated decision rule supports observation. 
CDC
+1

The evidence specifically supports observation for isolated vomiting: clinically important TBI is uncommon in children whose only PECARN-type symptom is vomiting. 
PEDIATRICS
+1

Because this is currently a clinic, if you cannot provide reliable serial neurologic observation and rapid access to CT/escalation, I would send him to a pediatric-capable ED for observation, rather than simply discharge him home at the 2-hour mark. 
Italian Journal of Pediatrics

Disposition

If he remains GCS 15, neurologically normal, behaves normally, tolerates PO, and has no further concerning symptoms through observation, discharge with a reliable caregiver is reasonable. Give explicit precautions for repeated vomiting, increasing sleepiness/difficulty awakening, seizure, confusion/abnormal behavior, worsening headache, weakness or impaired coordination, or loss of consciousness, any of which warrants immediate ED reassessment. 
CDC
+1

Practical plan: ED/appropriate monitored observation → no immediate CT → CT only for deterioration or persistent/worsening symptoms → discharge if stable through the observation period.
