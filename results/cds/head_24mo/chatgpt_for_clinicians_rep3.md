A 24-month-old boy is brought to clinic by his father after a fall at home.

About 2 hours ago he fell off the living-room couch onto a hardwood floor. Father was in the kitchen and heard the thud; the boy was crying when he got to him. He vomited once in the car on the way in. No seizure activity. Immunizations up to date. No drug allergies. Weight 12.8 kg. Otherwise healthy.

Exam: alert, walks to the toy bin and plays, GCS 15. HR 112, RR 24, SpO2 99% RA. 3 cm soft boggy swelling over the occiput, no step-off, no laceration. Pupils equal and reactive. No hemotympanum, no Battle sign, no periorbital bruising. Fontanelle closed. No bruising elsewhere on full skin exam. Neuro non-focal, gait normal. Remainder of exam unremarkable.

What is your plan?

Assessment

Minor blunt head trauma with a normal neurologic examination; I would favor monitored observation rather than immediate CT.

At 24 months, PECARN places him in the ≥2-year pathway. In that pathway, vomiting is an intermediate-risk predictor, whereas an occipital scalp hematoma is not itself a PECARN predictor once the child is ≥2 years old. High-risk features prompting CT—altered mental status/GCS <15 or signs of basilar skull fracture—are absent. 
The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health
+1

The age cutoff matters here: if he is actually younger than his second birthday, the occipital hematoma becomes an additional PECARN intermediate-risk feature. 
The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health

Management

Observe with serial neurologic examinations, rather than obtain an immediate head CT. A reasonable observation period for an intermediate-risk child is 4–6 hours from the time of injury; he is currently about 2 hours post-injury. 
Italian Journal of Pediatrics

Reassess mental status/GCS, pupils, gait/coordination, headache or irritability, vomiting, and the occipital swelling during observation. Observation before CT decision-making reduces CT use without evidence of increased missed clinically important TBI in appropriately selected children. 
Academic Emergency Medicine
+1

Escalate to noncontrast head CT if he develops recurrent/persistent vomiting, worsening headache or irritability, abnormal drowsiness/mental status, focal neurologic findings, seizure, or other clinical deterioration. 
CDC
+1

I would not obtain skull radiographs or routine MRI. Pediatric mTBI guidance recommends selective CT based on validated decision rules rather than routine imaging. 
CDC
+1

A single episode of vomiting in an otherwise neurologically normal child is particularly reassuring: clinically important TBI with isolated vomiting has been very uncommon in large pediatric cohorts, supporting observation instead of automatic CT. 
PEDIATRICS
+1

Disposition

If he remains GCS 15, neurologically normal, clinically improving, and without further concerning symptoms through observation, discharge with a reliable caregiver is reasonable. 
Italian Journal of Pediatrics

Give explicit return precautions for repeated vomiting, increasing somnolence or difficulty awakening, worsening headache/inconsolability, confusion or abnormal behavior, seizure, weakness or poor coordination, unequal pupils, or loss of consciousness. 
CDC
+1

Practical plan: observe another 2–4 hours, perform serial neuro checks, and avoid CT if he remains entirely well; obtain CT if symptoms recur or the examination deteriorates.
