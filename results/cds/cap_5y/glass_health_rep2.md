# Glass Health — Community-Acquired Pneumonia (5 Years) (Rep 2)

This presentation is most consistent with right-lower-lobe community-acquired pneumonia, with borderline hypoxemia and tachypnea requiring confirmation before outpatient treatment. Recheck pulse oximetry with a well-seated probe after antipyresis and at rest; given SpO₂ 93%, RR 38, focal findings, and maternal concern for tachypnea, I would have a low threshold for same-day ED evaluation if saturation remains ≤93%, work of breathing increases, or she cannot be observed reliably at home. SpO₂ <90% is a clear admission criterion, while some guidance uses higher thresholds. [1]





Immediate assessment




Repeat full vital signs after ibuprofen effect, including reliable room-air SpO₂ and respiratory rate while calm.
Assess hydration, perfusion, mental status, oral intake, and work of breathing serially.
Obtain a chest radiograph now if saturation remains low, respiratory status is concerning, or hospital referral is being considered; routine imaging is not required for an uncomplicated, well-appearing outpatient case, but is appropriate with admission concern or failure to improve. [2]




Treatment




If she remains alert, well perfused, drinking, without increased work of breathing, and SpO₂ is persistently ≥94%:





Start amoxicillin 90 mg/kg/day PO divided twice daily: for 18.5 kg, approximately 830 mg PO every 12 hours. Use the local suspension concentration and maximum-dose policy.
Treat for 5 days if clinically mild and improving, consistent with the current local protocol; extend or reassess if response is inadequate.
Continue fluids and age-appropriate nutrition.
Use weight-based acetaminophen or ibuprofen for discomfort/fever according to product labeling; avoid aspirin. No bronchodilator or corticosteroid is indicated without wheeze or another indication.




Disposition and follow-up




Reassess in clinic or by phone within 24–48 hours.
Send to the ED today for persistent SpO₂ ≤93%, SpO₂ <90% at any point, worsening tachypnea, retractions, cyanosis, inability to drink, repeated vomiting, lethargy, poor perfusion, or clinical deterioration.
If not clearly improving within 48–72 hours of antibiotics, reassess diagnosis, adherence, complications, and obtain chest imaging; consider ED/hospital evaluation.
