This is minor blunt head trauma with an intermediate-risk feature under PECARN, but I would not obtain an immediate head CT solely from the information given. The deliberate ambiguity in this case

The interesting feature is that 24 months sits exactly at the PECARN age cutoff. Moving the patient a few days across that boundary changes which finding counts: occipital hematoma matters in the <2 rule, whereas vomiting matters in the ≥2 rule. Despite that switch, this particular child lands in essentially the same practical decision space: observation versus CT, with observation quite reasonable given the reassuring examination and isolated/mild symptoms.

Assessment

This is minor blunt head trauma with a normal neurologic examination, but he is not PECARN very-low-risk because at exactly 24 months he falls into the “age ≥2 years” rule, and post-traumatic vomiting is an intermediate-risk predictor. The occipital hematoma is notable clinically, but nonfrontal scalp hematoma is a PECARN predictor only in children <2 years. 
The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health
+1

Management

Favor observation rather than immediate CT. He has GCS 15, no altered mental status, no basilar skull-fracture signs, no focal deficit, and the couch fall is not a PECARN severe mechanism assuming it was <5 ft. His single vomiting episode is therefore essentially an isolated intermediate-risk feature. PECARN supports observation versus CT, with observation particularly reasonable for isolated findings. 
ACEP
+1

I would observe until approximately 4–6 hours from the time of injury, with serial mental-status/GCS, pupils, gait/neurologic examination, and monitoring for recurrent vomiting, worsening headache, abnormal behavior, somnolence, or other deterioration.

Do not obtain a routine head CT now if he remains clinically well.
