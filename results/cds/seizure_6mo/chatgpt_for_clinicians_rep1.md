A 6-month-old girl is brought to clinic by her father after a shaking episode at home this morning.

She has had a runny nose and mild cough for 2 days. This morning while on the play mat she stiffened, then her arms and legs jerked rhythmically; father says both sides. He started timing partway through; his phone shows 9 minutes from when he began until it stopped on its own. She was sleepy for about 20 minutes afterward and has since nursed and is looking around. No vomiting, no rash. He thought she felt warm before the episode and gave acetaminophen after. Temperature at home 38.6°C (101.5°F). Clinic temperature is 38.9°C (102.0°F). No drug allergies. Weight 7.6 kg. Otherwise healthy.

Exam: alert, tracks, consolable, smiles at father. HR 142, RR 34, SpO2 99% RA. Anterior fontanelle soft and flat. Neck supple. TMs normal. Clear rhinorrhea. No rash, no petechiae. Moves all limbs symmetrically, tone normal, no focal findings. Remainder of exam unremarkable.

What is your plan?

Assessment

This is most consistent with a first generalized febrile seizure, but I would not confidently classify it as simple because the true duration is unknown: the father started timing partway through, and the recorded portion alone lasted 9 minutes. A simple febrile seizure must be generalized, occur once in 24 hours, and last <15 minutes; ≥15 minutes makes it complex. 
PEDIATRICS
+1

Given that she is exactly 6 months old, this is her first seizure, and the duration may have been prolonged, I would send her to a pediatric ED for further observation/evaluation rather than routine clinic discharge. Admission is not automatically required, but young age and possible complex seizure lower the threshold for observation. 
Pediatric Neurology

Workup

Repeat full neurologic examination and observe until she is clearly and persistently back to baseline.

Focus the evaluation on the source of fever; the current URI findings make a viral respiratory infection plausible. 
PEDIATRICS

Clarify specifically:

Hib and pneumococcal immunization status.

Any antibiotics in the preceding days.

Best estimate of seizure onset-to-offset duration.

Previous subtle seizures, developmental concerns, and family history of febrile seizures/epilepsy.

Lumbar puncture is not automatically required in a well-appearing infant with normal neurologic examination and no meningismus. However, because she is 6 months old, LP is an option if Hib/pneumococcal immunizations are incomplete or unknown, or if she has received antibiotics that could mask meningitis. Any clinical concern for meningitis/encephalitis should prompt LP. 
PEDIATRICS
+1

I would not routinely obtain CT/MRI or an emergent EEG solely because of this event if she remains neurologically normal; emergent intracranial pathology is uncommon even after a first complex febrile seizure without abnormal examination findings. 
Journal of the American College of Radiology
+1

Routine CBC/electrolytes are unnecessary for a clearly simple febrile seizure; if the event is ultimately considered complex, metabolic testing should be driven by persistent altered mental status, dehydration, concerning history, or other clinical findings. 
PEDIATRICS
+1

Management

She is no longer seizing, so no acute antiseizure medication is indicated now.

If another convulsive seizure lasts >5 minutes, treat promptly with an appropriate benzodiazepine and manage airway/respiratory status; seizures persisting beyond 5 minutes are less likely to terminate spontaneously. 
AAFP

Acetaminophen/ibuprofen can be used for comfort, but antipyretics should not be presented as prevention for another febrile seizure. 
PEDIATRICS

I would not start daily antiseizure prophylaxis after this isolated event. 
PEDIATRICS

Disposition / counseling

If she remains completely at neurologic baseline, has no recurrent seizure, no concerning infectious findings emerge, and the ED evaluation is reassuring, discharge with seizure first-aid counseling is reasonable. Caregivers should place her on her side on a safe surface, put nothing in her mouth, time the seizure, and call EMS for a seizure lasting >5 minutes, respiratory compromise/cyanosis, focal seizure activity, or failure to recover normally. Because this first event may have been prolonged, I would also consider whether a home rescue-medication plan is appropriate at discharge rather than treating this as an entirely routine simple febrile seizure. 
BMC Pediatrics
