A 5-year-old girl is brought to clinic by her mother for cough and fever.

She has had a cough for 3 days, worse at night, and fever for 2 days. Mother gave ibuprofen this morning. She is eating less but drinking well. No vomiting. Mother thinks she is breathing "a little fast." No history of wheezing. Clinic temperature is 38.8°C (101.8°F). Immunizations up to date. No drug allergies. Weight 18.5 kg. Otherwise healthy.

Exam: alert, mildly tired-appearing, talking in full sentences. HR 122, RR 38, SpO2 93% RA. No retractions, no nasal flaring. Crackles at the right base with decreased breath sounds there. No wheeze. Rest of chest clear. TMs normal. Throat mildly injected. Capillary refill under 2 seconds. Remainder of exam unremarkable.

What is your plan?

Assessment

This is most consistent with right lower-lobe community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), likely bacterial given fever plus focal crackles/decreased breath sounds. The management-changing finding is the SpO₂ of 93% on room air. Although she has no retractions and is speaking normally, 93% is borderline hypoxemia; pediatric CAP guidance notes that some clinicians hospitalize children with saturations as high as 93%, although the established admission threshold is sustained SpO₂ <90%. 
Clinical Infectious Diseases
+1

Workup

Repeat SpO₂ with a good waveform after she has settled and after antipyresis. I would not dismiss a reproducible 93%.

Obtain a chest radiograph today. Routine imaging is unnecessary for uncomplicated outpatient CAP, but it is recommended when hypoxemia is suspected. Here it is additionally useful because the decreased right-basilar breath sounds raise concern for a parapneumonic effusion. 
Clinical Infectious Diseases
+1

If CXR shows a moderate/large effusion, chest ultrasound is preferred over CT for characterization. 
Clinical Infectious Diseases
+1

No routine CBC, CRP, or blood culture if she remains nontoxic and can be managed outpatient. 
Clinical Infectious Diseases
+1

Management

My disposition would depend heavily on the repeat saturation:

If SpO₂ remains around 93% or falls, I would send her to a pediatric ED for imaging, observation, and determination of oxygen/admission need rather than treating this as routine clinic CAP. 
Clinical Infectious Diseases

If oxygenation clearly improves and remains reassuring, she continues to have minimal work of breathing, drinks well, and imaging shows uncomplicated pneumonia, outpatient treatment is reasonable.

For presumed uncomplicated bacterial CAP, amoxicillin is first-line; a commonly recommended outpatient regimen is 90 mg/kg/day PO divided BID. 
Clinical Infectious Diseases
+1
 A 5-day course is supported for uncomplicated pediatric CAP, with trials showing outcomes comparable to longer courses. 
JAMA Pediatrics
+2

I would not routinely add azithromycin solely because she is 5; reserve atypical coverage for a presentation genuinely suggestive of Mycoplasma or another atypical pathogen. 
AAFP

Fluids, antipyretic/analgesic as needed, rest; no bronchodilator without wheezing.

Monitoring

Recheck clinically within 24–48 hours, sooner if oxygenation is borderline. Failure to improve or deterioration over the next 48–72 hours warrants reassessment for resistant/alternative pathogens or complications such as effusion/empyema. 
American Family Physician

When to escalate

Immediate ED evaluation for increasing work of breathing, cyanosis, lethargy, inability to maintain hydration, or worsening/persistently low oxygen saturation. In this particular case, a reproducible room-air SpO₂ of 93% plus decreased right-basilar breath sounds is enough that I would favor same-day ED/imaging evaluation rather than simply prescribing amoxicillin and sending her home.
