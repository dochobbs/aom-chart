A 6-month-old girl is brought to clinic by her father after a shaking episode at home this morning.

She has had a runny nose and mild cough for 2 days. This morning while on the play mat she stiffened, then her arms and legs jerked rhythmically; father says both sides. He started timing partway through; his phone shows 9 minutes from when he began until it stopped on its own. She was sleepy for about 20 minutes afterward and has since nursed and is looking around. No vomiting, no rash. He thought she felt warm before the episode and gave acetaminophen after. Temperature at home 38.6°C (101.5°F). Clinic temperature is 38.9°C (102.0°F). No drug allergies. Weight 7.6 kg. Otherwise healthy.

Exam: alert, tracks, consolable, smiles at father. HR 142, RR 34, SpO2 99% RA. Anterior fontanelle soft and flat. Neck supple. TMs normal. Clear rhinorrhea. No rash, no petechiae. Moves all limbs symmetrically, tone normal, no focal findings. Remainder of exam unremarkable.

Assessment

This is most consistent with a first febrile seizure, likely triggered by a viral URI. However, I would not confidently classify it as a simple febrile seizure because the father began timing after the event had already started; the documented 9 minutes is therefore a minimum duration, and the actual seizure could have met the ≥15-minute criterion for a complex febrile seizure. Simple febrile seizures must be generalized, <15 minutes, and occur only once in 24 hours. 
PEDIATRICS
+2

Management

Same-day pediatric ED evaluation/observation. She is now neurologically normal and does not need emergent anticonvulsant treatment, but because this is her first seizure, she is exactly 6 months old, and the duration is uncertain, I would favor ED observation rather than immediate routine clinic discharge. Younger age and possible complex febrile seizure are commonly cited reasons for more cautious observation. 
Pediatric Neurology
+1

Look primarily for the source of fever and evidence of CNS infection. Her rhinorrhea/cough and reassuring examination favor a viral respiratory infection. Reassess mental status, fontanelle, neck, skin, hydration, and neurologic examination. Meningitis/encephalitis becomes substantially more concerning with persistent altered mental status, meningeal findings, petechiae/purpura, focal seizures/findings, or recurrent seizures. 
PEDIATRICS
+2

Lumbar puncture is not automatically required. In a well-appearing 6–12-month-old with a reassuring examination, routine LP is not recommended solely because this is a first febrile seizure. LP should be performed if history/examination suggests meningitis or intracranial infection. At this age it is also reasonable to consider LP if Hib/pneumococcal immunization is incomplete/unknown or if she was pretreated with systemic antibiotics. 
PEDIATRICS
+2

No routine head CT/MRI or emergent EEG if she remains at neurologic baseline without focal findings. Even with a complex febrile seizure, the neurologic examination should drive imaging decisions; clinically important imaging findings are uncommon without an abnormal/focal examination. Routine EEG has little acute-management value. 
Journal of the American College of Radiology
+3

Labs only if clinically indicated. Glucose/electrolytes are reasonable if there is persistent altered mental status, dehydration, vomiting/diarrhea, poor intake, or another metabolic concern; routine CBC/electrolytes are not needed merely because a febrile seizure occurred. Testing for the infectious source should be driven by the clinical picture. 
PEDIATRICS
+1

Treat fever for comfort, not to prevent another seizure. Antipyretics do not reliably prevent febrile-seizure recurrence. I would give the father seizure first-aid teaching: place her on her side/on a safe surface, do not restrain her or put anything in her mouth, and time the seizure from onset. A recurrent convulsive seizure lasting ≥5 minutes requires emergency treatment/EMS and an appropriate rescue-benzodiazepine pathway. 
PEDIATRICS
+2

Disposition: If ED observation confirms sustained return to baseline, no recurrent seizure, reassuring repeat examination, and no concerning infectious source, discharge with close pediatric follow-up is reasonable. If another seizure occurs within 24 hours, focality develops, mental status fails to normalize, or the history establishes a ≥15-minute event, manage as a complex febrile seizure and broaden the evaluation accordingly.
