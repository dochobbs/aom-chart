This presentation is most consistent with right lower-lobe community-acquired pneumonia, with borderline hypoxemia and tachypnea requiring confirmation before outpatient treatment.

Immediate reassessment
Repeat pulse oximetry with a reliable waveform, after rest and antipyretic; document room-air saturation and respiratory rate.
Assess hydration, work of breathing, mental status, and ability to ambulate/talk comfortably.
If SpO₂ remains 93% or lower, falls, or work of breathing increases, refer to the ED for oxygen assessment and possible observation/admission. Hospitalization is generally indicated below 90%, and some children with saturations around 93% warrant admission depending on trajectory and resources. 

Diagnostic evaluation
Obtain a chest radiograph today if available, given focal crackles/decreased breath sounds and low oxygen saturation; evaluate for focal consolidation or effusion.
Do not delay escalation for imaging if hypoxemia or respiratory distress persists. Routine viral testing is optional and should not delay treatment or disposition.
Consider respiratory viral testing if it will change isolation, cohorting, or antibiotic decisions.

Treatment
If repeat assessment shows stable oxygenation, no significant distress, reliable oral intake, and dependable follow-up:

Start amoxicillin 90 mg/kg/day PO divided twice daily. At 18.5 kg: approximately 830 mg PO twice daily; use the nearest practical formulation dose.
Treat for 5 days if clearly uncomplicated and clinically improving; extend or reassess if slow response, persistent fever, or complications are suspected.
Give fluids orally, rest, and weight-based acetaminophen or ibuprofen for discomfort/fever. Avoid cough-and-cold combination products.
No bronchodilator or corticosteroid is indicated without wheezing or another reactive-airway indication.

Follow-up and escalation
Recheck clinically within 24 hours, sooner if saturation is borderline or symptoms worsen.
Expect improvement in fever, respiratory rate, and activity within 48–72 hours.
Send to the ED immediately for increasing work of breathing, cyanosis, inability to drink, repeated vomiting, lethargy, confusion, apnea, chest pain, or persistent/worsening hypoxemia.
Failure to improve after 48–72 hours should prompt repeat examination, chest imaging, adherence review, and evaluation for effusion, resistant/atypical infection, or an alternative diagnosis. Imaging is particularly useful when outpatient treatment fails or complications are suspected.
