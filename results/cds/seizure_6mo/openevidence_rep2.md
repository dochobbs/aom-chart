This is a simple febrile seizure (generalized, single, and <15 minutes) in a well-appearing, now neurologically normal 6-month-old with a viral URI—but her age at the very youngest boundary of the febrile-seizure range (6–60 months) and any uncertainty about her Hib/pneumococcal immunization status are the two factors that should drive the plan.

Assessing for CNS infection is the central task. In well-appearing children with simple febrile seizures the risk of bacterial meningitis is very low (~0–0.86%), but meningeal signs are unreliable in infants and can be subtle.

Lumbar puncture is not mandatory here given the reassuring exam, but AAP guidance explicitly says LP should be considered for children 6–12 months of age with a febrile seizure when Hib and pneumococcal immunization is incomplete/uncertain, and strongly considered in any child pretreated with antibiotics. Some pediatric sources recommend LP more strongly in the first 12 months (and certainly under 6 months).

She received acetaminophen at home, not antibiotics, so partial treatment of meningitis is not a concern.

Immediate workup

Identify the fever source, not routine seizure labs—clear rhinorrhea with normal TMs suggests a viral URI. Well-appearing children with simple febrile seizures do not need routine CBC, electrolytes, glucose, cultures, neuroimaging, or EEG.

[2][7-8]

Check a urinalysis/urine culture in a 6-month-old girl with fever and no clear source, per standard febrile-infant practice, as UTI is the most common occult bacterial infection in this age/sex.

Reserve neuroimaging and EEG—not indicated for a simple febrile seizure with a normal neuro exam.

Red flags mandating LP ± broader sepsis workup and admission: any change to ill appearance, persistent lethargy/irritability beyond the brief postictal period, bulging/full fontanelle, neck stiffness, vomiting, non-blanching rash/petechiae, tachycardia out of proportion to fever, respiratory distress, seizure recurrence within 24 hours, focal or prolonged (>15 min) seizure, or persistently altered consciousness >1 hour.

Disposition and parent counseling for the father

Consider a period of observation in clinic/ED rather than immediate discharge—many sources favor a lower threshold for observation/admission in infants <18 months because differentiating from CNS infection is harder.

Reassure about prognosis: simple febrile seizures are benign, do not cause brain damage, and are not associated with adverse effects on later cognition, behavior, or academic performance; neither antipyretics nor daily antiepileptics prevent recurrence, so they are not indicated.
