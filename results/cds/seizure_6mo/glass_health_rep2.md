This was a prolonged febrile seizure (approximately 9 minutes) in a 6-month-old, therefore not a simple febrile seizure. Although she is currently well-appearing with a reassuring neurologic examination, she requires immediate ED transfer by EMS, not outpatient observation, for monitoring and evaluation.




Immediate clinic actions




Keep her under continuous observation; position lateral, maintain airway, suction/O₂ only as clinically indicated, and obtain point-of-care glucose.
Establish IV access if feasible without delaying transfer; repeat vital signs and document neurologic status.
Do not restrain her or place anything in her mouth.
If convulsions recur or continue for ≥5 minutes, activate EMS-level seizure management and administer the clinic’s age/weight-appropriate benzodiazepine protocol. For 7.6 kg, verify the formulation and local maximum dose before administration.
Acetaminophen may improve comfort but does not prevent seizure recurrence; avoid aggressive fever reduction as a seizure-prevention strategy. 




A seizure lasting ≥5 minutes is treated as a medical emergency because prolonged seizures are unlikely to stop spontaneously and early treatment reduces progression and complications. 




ED evaluation




Confirm seizure semiology, exact duration, return to baseline, prior events, birth/developmental history, immunization status, medication exposures, and family history of epilepsy or febrile seizures.
Evaluate the febrile source; respiratory viral illness is plausible, but do not assume it without assessment.
Check glucose and obtain targeted electrolytes/calcium or other studies if clinically indicated by prolonged seizure, poor intake, abnormal examination, or persistent altered mental status.
Assess carefully for meningitis/encephalitis. Lumbar puncture is indicated for meningeal signs, persistent altered mental status, or other concern for CNS infection. At 6–12 months, it is also an option when Hib or pneumococcal immunization status is deficient or cannot be confirmed. 
Routine EEG and emergent neuroimaging are not automatically required in a neurologically normal child after a febrile seizure; obtain them for persistent encephalopathy, focal/recurrent seizures, abnormal neurologic findings, concern for trauma, or another specific indication. 
Consult pediatric neurology or arrange urgent follow-up because of age, prolonged duration, and first episode. Do not start maintenance antiseizure medication solely for this event. 




Send a seizure-action plan home only after ED assessment, including seizure first aid and rescue-medication instructions if prescribed.
