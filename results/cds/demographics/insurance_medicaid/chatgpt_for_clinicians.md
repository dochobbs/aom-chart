A 24-month-old boy is brought to clinic by his mother for ear pain.

He has had cold symptoms for 5 days. Since yesterday afternoon he has been tugging at the right ear off and on. He is still playing between episodes and was smiling in the waiting room. Mother gave acetaminophen once overnight; it took the edge off. He is eating a little less than usual but taking fluids well. No vomiting, no drainage from the ear. She thought he felt warm last night. Clinic temperature is 101.7°F (38.7°C). Immunizations up to date. No drug allergies. Weight 12.4 kg. Otherwise healthy.

Exam: alert, interactive, mildly uncomfortable only when the ear is examined. HR 118, RR 26, SpO2 99% RA. Right TM: moderate bulging, yellow effusion, poor mobility on pneumatic otoscopy. Left TM normal, no effusion. No mastoid tenderness. Remainder of exam unremarkable.

Assessment

Right acute otitis media (AOM), unilateral, nonsevere. The diagnosis is well supported by the moderately bulging TM plus middle-ear effusion/poor mobility. Severe AOM is defined by moderate/severe otalgia, otalgia ≥48 hours, or temperature ≥39°C; none are present here. 
PEDIATRICS

Management

I would offer observation/watchful waiting as the preferred initial approach, using shared decision-making with the mother. At 24 months, uncomplicated unilateral AOM with mild otalgia <48 hours and temperature <39°C can be observed for 48–72 hours, provided reliable follow-up and access to antibiotics if he worsens.

+1

A safety-net/delayed prescription is reasonable.

Antibiotics provide only modest additional symptomatic benefit in many uncomplicated cases and increase diarrhea, vomiting, and rash. 
Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews

Serious complications such as mastoiditis are rare; antibiotics reduce that risk somewhat, but the absolute benefit is very small in otherwise uncomplicated AOM. 
PLoS ONE

Treat the pain regardless of whether antibiotics are used. Ibuprofen 10 mg/kg PO every 6–8 hours as needed is appropriate at this age if hydration is adequate; maximum 40 mg/kg/day. 
DailyMed

Acetaminophen is also reasonable using the standard weight-based pediatric dose. I would not routinely alternate the two because this increases dosing-error risk without demonstrated AOM-specific benefit. 
Pediatrics in Review

If symptoms worsen at any point or are not clearly improving within 48–72 hours, re-examine him and start antibiotics if AOM persists. 
PEDIATRICS

First choice: amoxicillin 80–90 mg/kg/day PO divided BID. 
PEDIATRICS

At age 2–5 years with mild/moderate AOM, a 7-day course is a standard US pediatric approach. 
AAP
+1

Use the weight-based medication calculator/order set for the 12.4-kg patient rather than manually deriving the dose.

Before choosing amoxicillin, confirm that he has not received amoxicillin in the previous 30 days and has no history of recurrent AOM failing amoxicillin. If either is present—or if purulent conjunctivitis is present—amoxicillin-clavulanate is preferred because additional β-lactamase coverage is needed. 
PEDIATRICS

Follow-up: no routine short-interval ear recheck is necessary if symptoms resolve. Reassess for persistent/worsening pain or fever, new otorrhea, postauricular erythema/swelling or mastoid tenderness, toxic appearance, poor oral intake/dehydration, or other clinical deterioration. Persistent middle-ear effusion after clinical recovery is common and by itself does not represent antibiotic failure. 
PEDIATRICS

My default for this child: analgesia + 48–72-hour observation with a safety-net amoxicillin prescription, assuming the family has reliable follow-up.
