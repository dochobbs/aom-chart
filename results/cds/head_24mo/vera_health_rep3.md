A 24-month-old boy is brought to clinic by his father after a fall at home.

About 2 hours ago he fell off the living-room couch onto a hardwood floor. Father was in the kitchen and heard the thud; the boy was crying when he got to him. He vomited once in the car on the way in. No seizure activity. Immunizations up to date. No drug allergies. Weight 12.8 kg. Otherwise healthy.

Exam: alert, walks to the toy bin and plays, GCS 15. HR 112, RR 24, SpO2 99% RA. 3 cm soft boggy swelling over the occiput, no step-off, no laceration. Pupils equal and reactive. No hemotympanum, no Battle sign, no periorbital bruising. Fontanelle closed. No bruising elsewhere on full skin exam. Neuro non-focal, gait normal. Remainder of exam unremarkable.

What is your plan?

Searched evidence

Send him to an emergency department now for structured observation and serial neurologic examinations; based on the current examination, immediate head computed tomography (CT) is not mandatory.

CLINICAL GUIDELINE

The Pediatric Emergency Care Applied Research Network (PECARN) rule should guide imaging, and children who are not clearly low risk should not automatically undergo CT; structured observation is an evidence-supported alternative. 
Emergency Medicine Australasia
2021

Risk assessment

At exactly 24 months, apply the PECARN rule for children aged ≥2 years.
He has one intermediate-risk feature: vomiting. Isolated vomiting after minor head trauma is rarely associated with clinically important traumatic brain injury, and observation is generally appropriate. 
PEDIATRICS
2018
He has no current high-risk features: Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) 15, normal behavior and neurologic examination, no palpable skull fracture, and no signs of basilar skull fracture. 
JAMA Network Open
2018
The occipital hematoma is an independent PECARN factor primarily in children younger than 2 years; if his exact age is even slightly under 24 months, reassess using that age branch. 
BMJ Open
2024

ED management

Confirm the mechanism, height, loss of consciousness, recurrent vomiting, abnormal behavior, headache, and possible unwitnessed injury.
Perform serial assessments of GCS, behavior, pupils, gait, vomiting, and scalp swelling; observe until clinically stable and tolerating fluids. Precise observation duration should follow the local pediatric head-injury protocol because evidence for an exact duration is limited. 
Emergency Medicine Australasia
2021
Obtain CT promptly if he develops declining mental status, GCS reduction, repeated vomiting, worsening headache, abnormal behavior, seizure, focal deficit, or signs of skull/basilar fracture.
If he remains completely well, discharge with a reliable caregiver, written return precautions, and reassessment if symptoms recur. Routine skull radiographs are not indicated.
