Searched evidence

This is most consistent with a first generalized febrile seizure, but because the father began timing after onset, the true duration is uncertain; I would arrange same-day pediatric emergency-department assessment and observation rather than discharge directly from clinic. A simple febrile seizure is generalized, occurs once in 24 hours, lasts <15 minutes, and is followed by return to baseline. This episode fits that pattern only if the total duration was <15 minutes and no focal features were missed.

CLINICAL GUIDELINE

Emergent neuroimaging is not recommended for a simple febrile seizure and is usually unnecessary even after a complex febrile seizure if the child returns to baseline and has no neurologic deficit. 
PEDIATRICS
2024

In clinic / emergency department

Reassess airway, breathing, circulation, temperature, hydration, neurologic status, and obtain a point-of-care glucose. Document the seizure semiology and establish whether it truly exceeded 15 minutes.
Identify the fever source. The upper-respiratory symptoms support a viral illness, but obtain urine testing if the respiratory source is not convincing, urinary symptoms develop, or fever remains unexplained.
Verify Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) and pneumococcal vaccination status. Lumbar puncture is not routine in an alert, well-appearing infant without meningismus, persistent altered consciousness, or sepsis; it should be considered with those findings, antibiotic pretreatment, or incomplete/unknown Hib or pneumococcal immunization at this age. Routine blood studies, electroencephalography (EEG), lumbar puncture, and neuroimaging are generally unnecessary after a typical simple febrile seizure. 
PEDIATRICS
2011
Observe until she is clearly at neurologic baseline, feeding normally, and no recurrent seizure occurs. Admit or obtain pediatric neurology input for recurrence, definite duration ≥15 minutes, focality, persistent postictal abnormality, abnormal examination, or unreliable follow-up.

Discharge if the evaluation remains reassuring

Provide seizure first-aid teaching: place her on her side, remove nearby hazards, do not restrain her or put anything in her mouth, and time any recurrence. Call emergency services if a seizure continues for 5 minutes or does not stop promptly; provide a prescribed rescue benzodiazepine plan if available.
Use acetaminophen for comfort and hydration, explaining that antipyretics do not prevent recurrent febrile seizures and chronic antiseizure medication is not indicated after a typical simple event. Antipyretics do not alter febrile-seizure recurrence, and routine long-term antiseizure therapy is not indicated.

Arrange prompt primary-care follow-up; obtain EEG or imaging only if later events are afebrile, focal, recurrent, prolonged, or development becomes abnormal.
