A 5-year-old girl is brought to clinic by her mother for cough and fever.

She has had a cough for 3 days, worse at night, and fever for 2 days. Mother gave ibuprofen this morning. She is eating less but drinking well. No vomiting. Mother thinks she is breathing "a little fast." No history of wheezing. Clinic temperature is 38.8°C (101.8°F). Immunizations up to date. No drug allergies. Weight 18.5 kg. Otherwise healthy.

Exam: alert, mildly tired-appearing, talking in full sentences. HR 122, RR 38, SpO2 93% RA. No retractions, no nasal flaring. Crackles at the right base with decreased breath sounds there. No wheeze. Rest of chest clear. TMs normal. Throat mildly injected. Capillary refill under 2 seconds. Remainder of exam unremarkable.

What is your plan?

Assessment

This is probable right lower-lobe community-acquired pneumonia, with fever, cough, focal crackles/decreased breath sounds, tachypnea, and borderline hypoxemia. The SpO₂ of 93% on room air is the management-changing feature; I would not simply diagnose uncomplicated CAP and discharge without reassessing oxygenation. Hypoxemia is an important predictor of CAP severity.

Workup

Repeat SpO₂ after ensuring a good waveform/probe position and after she has settled/received antipyretic therapy.

Given the 93% saturation plus focal decreased breath sounds, I would obtain a chest radiograph to assess the RLL and exclude a parapneumonic effusion. Pediatric CAP guidance recommends CXR when hypoxemia or significant respiratory distress is suspected, although routine films are unnecessary in straightforward outpatient CAP.

I would not routinely obtain CBC, CRP, procalcitonin, or blood culture if she remains well enough for outpatient management.

Influenza/COVID testing can be considered depending on current circulation and whether the result would change antiviral/isolation management. Viral infection does not exclude bacterial coinfection when the examination is this focal.

Management

Disposition depends heavily on the repeat saturation.
