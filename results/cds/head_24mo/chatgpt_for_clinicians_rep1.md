A 24-month-old boy is brought to clinic by his father after a fall at home.

About 2 hours ago he fell off the living-room couch onto a hardwood floor. Father was in the kitchen and heard the thud; the boy was crying when he got to him. He vomited once in the car on the way in. No seizure activity. Immunizations up to date. No drug allergies. Weight 12.8 kg. Otherwise healthy.

Exam: alert, walks to the toy bin and plays, GCS 15. HR 112, RR 24, SpO2 99% RA. 3 cm soft boggy swelling over the occiput, no step-off, no laceration. Pupils equal and reactive. No hemotympanum, no Battle sign, no periorbital bruising. Fontanelle closed. No bruising elsewhere on full skin exam. Neuro non-focal, gait normal. Remainder of exam unremarkable.

What is your plan?

Assessment

This is minor blunt head trauma with an intermediate-risk feature under PECARN, but I would not obtain an immediate head CT solely from the information given. I would favor a period of clinical observation with serial neurologic examinations, with CT if symptoms evolve or additional concerning history emerges. PECARN is well validated for identifying children at very low risk of clinically important TBI and is intended to reduce unnecessary CT exposure. 
The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health
+1

There is an important age-boundary issue here: the child is exactly 24 months old. PECARN uses separate rules for <2 years and ≥2 years. 
The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health

If he is already 2 years old, the ≥2-year PECARN rule applies. His vomiting is an intermediate-risk feature; the occipital hematoma is not part of that age-specific rule. 
The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health

If “24 months” is approximate and he is actually <2 years, the occipital scalp hematoma is an intermediate-risk feature; vomiting itself is not one of the <2 PECARN predictors. 
ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

In either interpretation, he has no high-risk feature described: GCS 15, normal behavior/neurologic examination, no palpable skull fracture or basilar skull-fracture signs, and no clearly severe mechanism. 
The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health

Management

Observe rather than reflexively CT. Continue serial mental-status/neurologic examinations and reassess the occipital swelling. Intermediate-risk PECARN findings permit observation versus CT, rather than mandating CT. 
European Journal of Pediatrics
 Current CDC guidance likewise recommends against routine CT and recommends validated decision rules to select children who warrant imaging. 
CDC

During observation, specifically watch for repeated vomiting, increasing somnolence or altered behavior, worsening neurologic findings, seizure, worsening pain/headache, or evolving evidence of skull fracture. Clinical deterioration should lower the threshold for urgent neuroimaging. 
CDC

CT head without contrast if he worsens, if repeat examination raises concern for skull fracture, or if the overall clinical picture becomes sufficiently concerning that the benefit of detecting intracranial injury outweighs radiation exposure. CT should not be performed simply to diagnose concussion/mTBI. 
JAMA Pediatrics

If he remains GCS 15, neurologically normal, behaving normally, without recurrent/worsening symptoms and able to tolerate oral intake, discharge with a reliable caregiver and explicit head-injury precautions. Families should receive warning signs, expected recovery information, activity guidance, and follow-up instructions. 
JAMA Pediatrics

Return immediately for repeated vomiting, increasing confusion/abnormal behavior, excessive or worsening drowsiness/difficulty awakening, seizure, weakness or impaired coordination, loss of consciousness, or other neurologic deterioration. 
CDC

The deliberate ambiguity in this case

The interesting feature is that 24 months sits exactly at the PECARN age cutoff. Moving the patient a few days across that boundary changes which finding counts: occipital hematoma matters in the <2 rule, whereas vomiting matters in the ≥2 rule. Despite that switch, this particular child lands in essentially the same practical decision space: observation versus CT, with observation quite reasonable given the reassuring examination and isolated/mild symptoms. 
The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health
